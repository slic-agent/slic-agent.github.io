Motivation and task

Toward reasoning-driven analog circuit design

Analog integrated circuit design requires repeated decisions over coupled performance trade-offs, often guided by human intuition and hand-crafted tuning. Learning this expertise from examples alone would require broad design coverage. Yet public circuit data are scarce, and fabrication-process models and many circuit designs are proprietary.

These constraints motivate a small, locally deployable reasoning language model. When confidentiality requirements preclude hosted LLM services, it can keep sensitive circuit and process data in-house while exposing human-interpretable reasoning and interactive parameter updates that engineers can inspect. Compared with purely numerical search, such as Bayesian optimization, this approach also exposes the rationale behind proposed changes and supports an interactive design process.

Our hypothesis. Some circuit knowledge and reasoning strategies can transfer across circuit topologies and fabrication processes (commonly described by their technology nodes). Learning these strategies could help a model generalize to circuit topologies and fabrication processes not seen during training.

SLIC combines a small set of demonstrations with reinforcement learning from verifiable simulator feedback. Simulation provides both new experience and a measurable learning signal, enabling a local 4B model to practice successive design decisions without requiring a large proprietary design corpus.

The task: turn an amplifier into a working design

Given an analog amplifier and design targets, adjust the circuit so that it satisfies all targets together. The circuit connectivity is fixed within each task. The model proposes parameter updates, runs a circuit simulator, and uses measured simulation results to decide what to change next.

Given

An amplifier topology, its initial state, a technology model, and design targets.

Decide

Which circuit parameters to change, by how much, and how many updates to make before the next simulation.

Succeed when

The simulated circuit meets gain, bandwidth, power, and phase-margin requirements together within the simulator-call budget.

Method

From demonstrations to learning through simulation

Supervised fine-tuning teaches a starting behavior. Reinforcement learning develops iterative design strategies.

01

Supervised fine-tuning: learn the basics from a teacher

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash collects simulator-grounded trajectories on public PTM 65 nm circuits. Qwen3.5-4B learns from selected single-turn examples.

  1. Collect

    Explore without explicit goals

    A strong teacher proposes parameter updates and observes the simulated outcomes.

  2. Relabel

    Use achieved performance as the goal

    The best-observed performance tuple becomes the trajectory’s target specification.

  3. Filter and teach

    Learn from useful individual turns

    Retain valid turns from successful episodes and valid, improving turns from unsuccessful ones, each with reasoning.

Source: manuscript Figure 1 and Section 3.1. Simplified SFT data-construction schematic.

6,981 single-turn samples from 1,568 teacher episodes: 6,233 training samples and 748 held-out samples. Episodes are kept separate across the two splits; both cover the same 35 topologies.

Teacher–student analogy. The demonstrations teach a student the basic circuit reasoning, response format, and parameter-update language before it practices making successive decisions on its own.

02

Reinforcement learning: practice with simulator feedback

The SFT-initialized model learns from verifiable simulator feedback over rollouts of up to eight turns.

RGRS: online rollout construction

Sample and simulate alternatives, continue from one eligible state, and retain all candidates for policy learning.

Domain-validity gates

Invalid amplification cannot earn misleading performance credit. Gain and frequency-response checks establish whether the design is meaningful before rewarding its specifications.

Separate normalization

Validity and performance signals are normalized separately so that invalid-action penalties do not overwhelm differences among measured designs.

Success-conditional slack bonus

Once all targets are satisfied, additional performance headroom continues to provide a learning signal, even in all-success groups.

Structured state and relative-update actions +

Each prompt includes circuit connectivity, design targets, device parameters, operating-point measurements, and admissible actions. Matched devices share parameters. A single interaction turn may contain several parameter changes before the next simulation.

Generalization across technology nodes

A policy trained at PTM 65 nm remains effective across five unseen nodes without additional fine-tuning. SLIC–Softmax achieves 61.9–71.0% held-out-topology success across PTM 22, 32, 45, 90, and 130 nm, compared with 72.6% at the training node. The same training process also supports transfer to a commercial foundry PDK, evaluated below.

Practical deployment on a commercial PDK. Without additional fine-tuning, SLIC–Random reaches 55.8% success on seen topologies and 49.9% on unseen topologies using TSMC 65 nm CMOS. Local inference and Spectre simulation keep proprietary process models and circuit data within the local environment, without sending them to hosted LLM services.

A commercial foundry PDK is a more detailed practical design environment than the public PTM models used for training, even at the same nominal 65 nm node. Further local RL offers a route to adapt the policy to that environment and potentially improve performance; the results above measure transfer before such adaptation.

Test-time scaling with simulator feedback

We extend the evaluation budget to 32 simulator calls and compare policies trained with different rollout horizons. The best-performing variants reach 95.3% success on seen topologies and 83.2% on unseen topologies.

Interpretation. Four-turn training preserves continued improvement at evaluation time, whereas single-turn training saturates earlier. A single-turn training episode provides no experience of revising a design after feedback. These results suggest that a moderate training horizon can retain iterative refinement while reducing training cost.

Tolerance sweep: performance under stricter targets

The gains persist when evaluation requires strict target satisfaction. At 0% tolerance, SLIC–Random reaches 71.9% ID success and SLIC–Softmax reaches 59.3% OOD success. Across the 0–15% evaluation sweep, Random trained with the default 5% success tolerance outperforms its zero-tolerance training counterpart, including at strict 0% evaluation.

Interpretation. Modest training tolerance may make successful outcomes easier to reach early in learning, while the success-conditional slack bonus continues to encourage headroom.

Training dynamics: response length alone does not explain success

Greedy develops the longest responses but remains less successful than Random and Softmax. Response length alone is therefore an incomplete indicator of effective use of simulator feedback.

Observation. More generated reasoning does not necessarily yield better circuit refinement. In the sampled responses, Greedy repeatedly reconsiders its decisions, while Softmax more often connects simulator feedback to a diagnosis, proposed updates, and expected effects.
